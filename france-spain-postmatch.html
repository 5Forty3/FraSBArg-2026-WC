We called it a coin-flip and leaned narrowly to France. Spain answered by producing their most complete performance of the tournament — and it wasn't close.

🎯 The prediction, and the reality

Our pre-match analysis framed this as a coin-flip hinging on the midfield war — "if Spain's pair dictate the tempo, France get pinned back and their front three starve" — and leaned, narrowly, to France's ruthlessness. The first half of that sentence proved exactly right; the lean did not. Rodri and Fabián did not merely win the midfield, they annexed it. France's front three starved so completely that Unai Simón did not make a save until the 64th minute. The high line we flagged as "a mortal risk" against Mbappé instead became the instrument of his execution. Full credit to Spain: they answered every question we posed, and did it with the ball at their feet.

There are nights when football stops being a contest of athletes and becomes an argument between ideas — and in Dallas, one idea won so comprehensively that the other never got a word in. Spain played a purely technical game: the perfect passing, the triangles and quadrangles forming and dissolving in midfield, and, most impressively of all, an absolute defensive shape that never gave the most feared attack in world football an inch of space anywhere near the penalty area. This was not a smash-and-grab or a backs-to-the-wall rearguard. This was a demonstration. France — 16 goals in six games, Mbappé and Dembélé and Olise, the most explosive front line on earth — were rendered, for an hour, entirely mute.

And there is a delicious irony in the manner of it. It is a testament to Spain's miserliness of technique — that peculiar Spanish habit of passing the ball so beautifully that they occasionally forget another team is playing at all — that the only two shots on target in this match were the two goals Spain scored. Until the hour mark, that was the entire ledger of the World Cup semi-final between the world's number one and number three: two shots on target, two goals, both Spanish. France's first meaningful test of Unai Simón's palms did not arrive until the 64th minute.

Spain pass the ball so beautifully that they sometimes forget there is another team on the pitch. Until the hour, the only two shots on target in a World Cup semi-final were the two goals Spain had scored. That is not dominance. That is a different sport being played by one side. — The miserliness of technique

🕸️ How Mbappé Was Erased — the high line as a weapon

Spain · The LineCubarsí, Laporte and a millimetre-perfect trap that swallowed the world's fastest man
Spain's high line, stepping as one France's release ball — caught offside

This is the tactical heart of the night, and it was executed with almost cruel precision. We wrote before the match that Spain's high line was "a mortal risk" against Mbappé's pace. It proved the opposite: the high back line Spain deployed was so brilliantly, millimetrically accurate that it almost always caught the super-pacy Mbappé — stepping as one unit on the trigger of the pass, turning his greatest weapon into his cage. And behind it, Unai Simón operated as an aggressive sweeper, racing yards outside his box to slide in and snuff out a breakaway before Mbappé could settle the ball. The result was total erasure: the Golden Boot co-leader was simply absent from the match until the hour mark, when he finally produced his first trademark run from the left — and Simón was quick enough to react and parry it behind. Pace is a weapon only if there is space to run into. Spain, quite simply, deleted the space.

🚫 No Air to Breathe — the shape that suffocated a front five

Spain · The ShapeNot an inch near the box — the most feared attack in football, denied oxygen
Spain's compact block French passing lanes — cut

And if Mbappé was patchy, the rest of the mighty French front line was worse — because Spain's shape gave none of them a breath of air. Ousmane Dembélé — the reigning Ballon d'Or holder, brilliant one day and absent the next, as he has been his entire career — was utterly missing: not a single cross into the box all night. Barcola and Olise were so ineffective that both had to be replaced; Deschamps shuffled Olise to the right and Dembélé into a central No.10 role at half-time in a desperate search for a spark that never came. The reason is drawn above: Spain's block left no gaps between the lines, Rodri and Fabián screened every entry pass, and the full-backs tucked in to deny the half-spaces. France's celebrated ability to create offence out of thin air met a team that had removed the air from the stadium. A front five that had scored sixteen goals in six games managed nothing of substance for an hour.

🔺 Triangles and Quadrangles — the tiki-taka returns

Spain · The GeometryRodri, Fabián, Olmo, Yamal — the shapes that made a semi-final look like a training drill
Spain's circulation — triangles & quadrangles

This is what a controlled World Cup semi-final looks like when one team has the technical keys to it. Spain were effective and highly technical, and at moments they showed genuine, gorgeous tiki-taka — the old religion, briefly resurrected: Yamal, Olmo and Fabián Ruiz combining in a one-touch sequence inside the box that the broadcast rightly called the beautiful game on full display. The mechanism is the geometry above: triangles forming around Rodri, dissolving, reforming as quadrangles further up; every player an angle for the man on the ball; France's midfielders chasing shapes rather than opponents. It can best be described as easing themselves to victory. And presiding over it all was Rodri, outstanding once more — protecting his defence, screening every counter at source, and dictating the tempo with the same untouchable authority no side has managed to disturb all tournament. France's entire identity is the transition — the moment everything changes in an instant. Spain's midfield technicality never let the moment exist.

⚽ The Second Goal (58') — Olmo, Porro and the give-and-go that killed it

Spain · 2-0A textbook 1-2 — Olmo the creator, Porro the finisher, France caught ball-watching
Olmo & Porro's 1-2 Porro's untracked run The finish

The goal that ended the contest was, fittingly, a piece of pure technical football — and a French lapse. Dani Olmo, brilliant throughout as he has been all tournament, was the main creator: Pedro Porro fed it into him and continued his run; Olmo, with the awareness that has defined his campaign, laid a sweet pass back into the full-back's path in a classic give-and-go; Porro arrived and beat Maignan. The French error was glaring — Désiré Doué, just introduced from the bench, was marking Porro, watched the ball go and did not follow the run, and no French player picked him up. The players' faces afterwards said everything: someone had blown an assignment. But note the deeper point — even Spain's full-back was an attacking weapon, arriving in the box because the geometry invited him there. This is what happens when a team's shape means everyone is a passing option and everyone is a runner. Three minutes later, Yamal — full of tricks all night — produced a beautiful finish that was chalked off for one of the tightest offsides you will see. Mercy, of a sort.

😤 The moment that captured it all — Mbappé's yellow card

Late on, with France's attack devoid of ideas and inspiration, the frustration boiled over into a single, perfectly emblematic image: Kylian Mbappé, the world's most dangerous forward, chasing a hopeless ball into the Spanish goalmouth and needlessly fouling Unai Simón — and collecting a yellow card for it. There was nothing to be gained; the ball was gone; the game was gone. It was the act of a great player reduced to flailing at a problem he could not solve, on a night when everything he does best had been methodically dismantled. The broadcasters reached for the very same image: France's frustration, one wrote, was best summarised by Mbappé being booked for a foul on the goalkeeper. When the Golden Boot leader's defining contribution is a cynical foul on the opposition keeper, the tactical argument is already over.

🎭 The Grand Argument — pace and power vs puritanical football

Step back and this becomes the most interesting tactical fixture of the modern game, played twice now with the same result. France were completely outplayed — again — exactly as they were two years ago in the Euro 2024 semi-final. This is a recurring, and revealing, order of battle: arguably the best attacking team in the world right now meets the best technical team in the world, and fails utterly. Twice. France's greatness flows from transition play — the ability to change everything in a single moment, to turn a stray pass into a goal in six seconds. But transition requires a moment of chaos to exploit, and this is precisely where Spain's midfield technicality intervenes: they simply never surrender the moment. Rodri screens, the triangles reform, the ball comes back, and the chaos France need never arrives. There, laid bare, is a pure clash of footballing philosophy — and the verdict is stark: pace, power and attacking force alone cannot win you World Cups against puritanical football. For a purist, that is genuinely heartening. The idea won.

Arguably the best attacking team on earth met the best technical team on earth — and failed completely, for the second time in two years. Pace and power alone cannot beat puritanical football. For the purists, that is a heartening night. — The philosophy, vindicated

📋 The Performers

Rodri — Outstanding, again. Protected the defence, screened every French transition at source, and dictated tempo untouched. The central character of the Armada, as ever.
Dani Olmo — Brilliant, as all tournament. The main creator of the second goal with a sublime lay-off, plus a back-heel that nearly made another. Spain's most consistently excellent player.
Marc Cucurella — At his best, both ways. Superb going forward and utterly reliable at the back — the complete full-back display we've been waiting for.
Lamine Yamal — Full of tricks. Won the penalty, tormented Digne all night, and had a beautiful goal disallowed for the tightest of offsides. Turned 19 the day before.
Unai Simón — Sweeper and saviour. Raced from his box to snuff out a breakaway, then made his first actual save on 64 minutes. Now 650+ minutes without conceding — a World Cup record.
Ousmane Dembélé — Absent. The reigning Ballon d'Or winner, inconsistent as ever across his career — brilliant one day, missing the next. Tonight: not a single cross into the box.
Kylian Mbappé — Erased, then frustrated. Caught by the high line all night, absent until the hour, and his lasting contribution a needless yellow card for fouling Simón.
Barcola & Olise — Both replaced. Deschamps' pace-over-polish gamble on Barcola never fired; Olise was shuffled and then withdrawn. No spark from either flank.

🔮 And Still Not Their Best — the frightening thought

Here is the observation that should terrify whoever emerges from the other semi-final. As we have analysed repeatedly, Spain are still not playing at their best. They remain the team that creates far more than it converts, that eases rather than annihilates, that keeps something in reserve. Neither was tonight their absolute ceiling. And yet this was, by some distance, the most perfect match they have played in this tournament — a complete, flawless dismantling of the highest-ranked team on the planet and the most feared attack in football, without ever appearing to strain. That is quite some saying. Imagine what happens when they finally play to their full potential. Spain have now conceded a single goal in the entire tournament; Unai Simón holds the World Cup record for minutes without conceding at 650 and counting; and two more clean sheets would make them the meanest champions in the history of the competition. One match stands between them and that.

🏆 The Final — Who's Waiting at MetLife?

Spain now await the winner of England vs Argentina in Atlanta. The two possible finals could hardly be more different in character — and both, in their way, would test whether this Spanish idea can complete the job.

Final Scenario A🇪🇸 Spain vs 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 England — the control derby

A Spain-England final would be the more one-sided of the two on current form. England have been thoroughly unconvincing — outplayed for long spells by Norway, dependent on Bellingham's individual brilliance, and publicly criticised by their own manager. They would almost certainly cede the ball and try to defend deep, hunt set-pieces, and drag it toward penalties. But that is precisely the game Spain have beaten all tournament — Portugal, Belgium and now France all tried variations of it. The Rodri-vs-Rice midfield contrast would be stark, and Yamal against England's left side a serious mismatch. Spain would be heavy favourites; England's hope would rest on the two things they genuinely do have: dead-ball threat, and a talisman capable of settling any game on his own.

Final Scenario B🇪🇸 Spain vs 🇦🇷 Argentina — technique vs the magician

A Spain-Argentina final would be the richer, more finely balanced contest — and the one the neutrals want. It would pit the tournament's most complete technical machine against its most stubborn, magician-led survivors: Rodri's screen against Messi's pockets, Spain's geometry against Argentina's control of time. The tactical crux is clear — Spain's method is to deny the opponent the ball entirely, and Argentina, as we saw against Switzerland, have no Plan B once Messi is starved. If Rodri and Cubarsí do to Messi what they did to Mbappé, Argentina's aged legs would have nothing left to offer. But Messi is not Mbappé; he needs no space to run into, only a half-yard to think in — and Spain's chronic wastefulness in front of goal would leave the door ajar for exactly the kind of late, improbable moment Argentina have made their signature. Add Dibu Martínez waiting in any shootout, and you have a genuine final. Spain would be favoured — but only narrowly, and never comfortably.

📊 The Last Word

Spain are in the World Cup final, and they got there by winning an argument, not just a football match. This was a purely technical performance — perfect passing, triangles and quadrangles, and a defensive shape that denied the most feared attack on earth a single inch near the box. The high line caught Mbappé so relentlessly that he did not exist until the hour; Dembélé, inconsistent as ever, managed not one cross; Barcola and Olise were withdrawn; and France's frustration distilled into their captain's needless yellow card for fouling Unai Simón. It was, remarkably, a repeat of the Euro 2024 semi-final — the best attacking team in the world meeting the best technical team in the world and failing, completely, for a second time. Pace and power cannot beat puritanical football, and for a purist that is a beautiful thing to know. Olmo was brilliant, Cucurella complete, Yamal irrepressible, and Rodri untouchable. And the most chilling part: this was Spain's most perfect match of the tournament, and still not their best. Imagine the ceiling. One more to go. Vamos España.

🎯 The prediction, and the reality

Our pre-match analysis called it a tight, low-scoring Spanish win — a 1-0 settled by a Yamal or Oyarzabal moment, with Merino's bench impact flagged from the Portugal game. The scoreline finished one goal busier at each end, but the essence held perfectly: Spain dominated (xG 2.08–0.38, 16 shots to 5), laboured for long spells, conceded their first goal of the tournament, and were rescued late by Merino off the bench — his second consecutive winner as a substitute. Everything we said about Spain's control-without-ruthlessness, and about a Courtois-shaped obstacle, came to pass. They are through. But this was the win of a team that keeps finding a way rather than blowing anyone away.

There is a peculiar tension at the heart of this Spain side, and ninety-plus minutes in Los Angeles laid it bare once more. Here is a team of extravagant, generational talent — arguably the deepest well of technical quality in the tournament — and yet it plays as though some invisible hand restrains it, as though it is forever winning matches at seventy per cent of what it could be. Belgium, a strong and proud opponent for all their years, were beaten 2-1. But it was a tiring, laboured victory over an ageing team, decided not by a flood of the brilliance Spain so obviously possess, but by a substitute turning in a rebound at the death. Good teams win these. Great teams, with this much ability, are supposed to make them look easier than this.

And yet — and this is the paradox — winning ugly is itself a kind of greatness, and it is the greatness that lifts trophies. The heartbreak, for a purist, is the sense of what is being left on the table. So much raw talent, so little abandon. But Spain keep advancing, and they advance now to the tie of the tournament.

🧭 Rodri — The Central Character of the Armada

If one man explained this match, it was Rodri. Once again he was the central character of the Spanish Armada — the fulcrum around which everything turns. It is Rodri who builds from the back, who receives under pressure and, crucially, who takes the game vertical when a lesser controller would only recycle it sideways. All tournament, the story has been the same: no opponent has yet found a way to stifle him, to force him into purely horizontal, sterile possession. Belgium tried, harrying him through Witsel and Vanaken, and still he dictated — the metronome setting Spain's tempo, the platform for their control, and the reason they concede so rarely. He is the least flashy of Spain's stars and the most indispensable.

Nobody has managed to stifle Rodri into the horizontal all tournament — to stop him taking the game forward. That single duel, more than any other, is what will make Spain versus France so fascinating: because France, of anyone left, have the engine and the athleticism to try. — The battle that may decide the semi-final

This is the mechanism that makes Spain tick — and Rodri is the only one who can run it. Possession-heavy sides live or die by whether their deepest midfielder can progress the ball forward or merely keep it moving side to side. Rodri, uniquely, does the former under the heaviest pressure: he absorbs the press, shields the ball, and picks the vertical pass — into Pedri's feet, out to Yamal, through a line — that turns sterile control into genuine threat. Belgium's whole midfield plan was to deny him that, and they couldn't. It is why Spain have conceded so little (their rest-defence starts with him) and created so much. The tantalising question, now, is France — because in Tchouaméni (if fit), Koné and Rabiot, Les Bleus have the athletic, ball-winning engine most likely to finally make Rodri go sideways. Win that duel, and Spain control the semi. Lose it, and they may be forced into exactly the passive, horizontal football that has always been the ceiling on this team.

⚽ The Opener (30') — Fabián Ruiz pounces on the rebound

Spain · 1-0Courtois parries, Fabián Ruiz is quickest to react
Initial shot, parried Fabián Ruiz finishes the rebound

A goal born of persistence rather than a flourish — fittingly for this Spain. On the half-hour, Spain's pressure finally forced an opening: a shot that even the great Thibaut Courtois could only parry, and Fabián Ruiz was sharpest to the rebound, tucking it away to reward the sustained siege. It was, tellingly, the first of two Spanish goals that came from a saved shot dropping to a Spaniard who didn't miss the second time — a sign both of relentless pressure and of a finishing touch that needed a second bite. For half an hour Spain had knocked and knocked; now the door creaked open. And with the tournament's meanest defence behind them, a 1-0 lead felt, briefly, like it might be all they needed.

⚽ The Reply (41') — De Ketelaere ends the record

Belgium · 1-1The first goal Spain conceded all tournament — 10+ hours ended
Belgium's break De Ketelaere's finish

A moment of genuine history, and a jolt to Spanish nerves. Just before half-time, Belgium did what no team had managed in more than ten hours of football: they scored against Spain. Charles De Ketelaere — Belgium's in-form forward — finished to make it 1-1, ending Spain's World Cup-record run of six consecutive clean sheets and their unbeaten-goal streak in a single stroke. It was a reminder that even the meanest defence is not impregnable, and that Belgium, for all their limitations, carried the individual quality to punish a rare lapse. For a side that had drawn so much confidence from never conceding, the psychological jolt was real — and it forced Spain to do something they hadn't all tournament: come from level, late, against a stubborn opponent. Their answer, once again, came from the bench.

⚽ The Winner (88') — Merino, off the bench, again

Spain · 2-1Lammens spills Cubarsí's shot — Merino is there, as he always is
Cubarsí's shot, spilled Merino's winner

Different opponent, same script — and the same hero. With the game drifting toward extra time, De la Fuente turned again to Mikel Merino, sending him on for Olmo. Within roughly two minutes he had won the tie: Pau Cubarsí drove in a shot, Belgium's substitute goalkeeper Senne Lammens — on for the injured Courtois — could only spill it, and Merino was there to turn home the rebound in the 88th minute. It was his second consecutive game-winning goal off the bench, after the identical late act against Portugal four days earlier. There is a lesson buried in it: for all the questions about Spain's starters, their bench and their pragmatism keep delivering exactly when required. It was fitting, too, that both Spanish goals came from a spilled save — a night of relentless pressure that finally, twice, broke through on the second attempt. Cruel on Belgium; deserved, on the balance of play, by Spain.

🩹 A note on Belgium's misfortune — and their farewell

Belgium were dealt a brutal hand. They lost captain Youri Tielemans to an injury in the warm-up, and then Thibaut Courtois — imperious as ever, denying Yamal and Oyarzabal repeatedly — went off injured on 73 minutes, forcing on the untested Lammens whose spill decided the tie. Whether Courtois stays on and saves Cubarsí's shot is one of the game's great what-ifs. Producing just 0.38 xG, Belgium rode their luck and their goalkeeper for long spells, but they defended with the resilience their name demands. This, in all likelihood, is the final bow of Belgium's "Golden Generation" — De Bruyne, Lukaku and company — a group of immense talent that, agonisingly, never quite won the prize their gifts promised. They deserve respect on the way out.

🔬 The Individuals — Talent, and the Missing Spark

The collective story was clear, but the individual picture explains why this Spain intrigues and frustrates in equal measure. It is a squad brimming with talent that, player by player, seems to be operating just below its own summit.

Rodri — The indispensable one. The central character again: built from the back, took it vertical, and was never stifled into sideways football. Spain's engine and their difference-maker.
Lamine Yamal — Unstoppable, until the finish. Almost impossible to contain around the box — beat men at will — but he still seems to lack the confidence to end his dazzling dribbles with a decisive strike. One goal all tournament for all his brilliance.
Pedri — Gifted, but muted. Still elegant on the ball, yet the incisive, line-splitting through-balls that define him at his best have gone quiet. The vision is there; the killer pass, less so.
Cucurella — A tale of two halves. Arguably the best left-back in the tournament, yet virtually absent going forward in the first 45 — then far more influential after the break. Consistency, again, the theme.
Baena & Nico (sub) — Willing foils, inconsistent. A good balance on the left to Yamal's right, but neither has strung together the sustained end-product to fully unlock this side.
Mikel Merino — The super-sub, again. On for Olmo, decisive within two minutes. Two straight game-winners off the bench. Spain's pragmatic ace in the hole.
Yamal is almost impossible to stop around the goal — but he plays as though something restrains him from finishing his artistry with a strike. Pedri's vision flickers where it once blazed. Cucurella arrives only after the interval. It is the story of the whole team in miniature: an abundance of talent, and an invisible hand holding it back from true greatness. — So much gifted, so little unleashed
🎭 The Philosophy — De la Fuente's Pragmatism vs the Purist's Dream

Here is the deeper truth this tournament keeps revealing about Spain, and it is one that will divide hearts. This is not the Spain of the tiki-taka gospel — not a side bent on hypnotic, total domination of the ball as an end in itself. Under Luis de la Fuente, Spain have become something more pragmatic: a team that controls, defends ferociously, absorbs a blow, and finds a way to win in a laboured, unglamorous fashion — often through a substitute, often late. For the purist, there is real heartbreak in this. One thinks of Luis Enrique's Spain — bolder, more romantic, committed to the beautiful ideal — which for all its aesthetic courage did not lift the biggest prizes. And here is De la Fuente, less doctrinaire, arguably less thrilling, winning a European Championship and now marching through a World Cup by the simple, ruthless expedient of winning matches. It breaks a romantic's heart that the pragmatist succeeds where the idealist fell short. But football, in the end, remembers the trophies — and this method, for all that it leaves talent unspent, keeps working.

🔮 The Future — Spain vs France: The Tie of the Tournament

Semi-Final · Dallas, Jul 14Two of the world's most talented teams, one final place

And so we arrive at the tie the whole tournament has been building toward: Spain vs France, in Dallas, for a place in the final. It is, on paper, the clash of the two most talented teams left standing — and a fascinating collision of philosophies. Spain will want to do to France what they've done to everyone: monopolise the ball, control through Rodri and Pedri, and strangle the game. But France are the one side with the athletic midfield engine — Tchouaméni (fitness permitting), Koné, Rabiot — capable of pressing Spain, contesting Rodri, and forcing the horizontal, sterile possession that is this Spain's ceiling. And where Spain labour to finish, France are lethally efficient: Mbappé (8 goals, level with Messi), Dembélé and Doué will punish any of the openings Spain's high line concedes on the counter. The defining questions: can Spain's control survive France's press, and can Spain's profligacy survive against a side that needs only a half-chance? If Spain play at their theoretical peak, they can win — their defence and midfield are that good. But on the evidence of this laboured tournament, they must finally unleash the talent they keep holding back. Against France, 70 per cent will not be enough. Vamos España — but this time, no restraint.

✦ The FraSBArg scorecard — three down to two, one to go

A moment to take stock of our original quartet. France and Spain have both come through to the semi-finals with flying colours — France clinical and imperious, Spain resilient and effective. Argentina, our third survivor, march on too after their miraculous comeback against Egypt, awaiting their quarter-final with Switzerland. Only Brazil — always the weak link of the four — has fallen, undone by Norway. Now comes the cruel arithmetic of the bracket: because France and Spain sit in the same half, this semi-final guarantees that one of our teams goes out. Two of the quartet have passed the test; one more still stands alone in the other half. Whatever happens in Dallas, the dream of a FraSBArg final lives on through Argentina — and, for one of France or Spain, through to the last act at MetLife.

📊 The Last Word

Spain are into their first World Cup semi-final since their triumphant 2010, and they got there exactly as this tournament has taught us to expect: dominant on the numbers, laboured in the flesh, and rescued by Mikel Merino off the bench for the second game running. Belgium — brave, unlucky with injuries to Tielemans and Courtois, and bidding a poignant farewell to their Golden Generation — were beaten but far from disgraced. The abiding image of Spain remains a paradox: a team of breathtaking talent that plays as though something restrains it, winning in a pragmatic, unglamorous style that would break a purist's heart even as it keeps delivering results. Rodri was again the central character, untouchable in his control; Yamal dazzled without finishing; Pedri and Cucurella flickered rather than blazed. It has been enough. Now comes France, and a semi-final that will demand every ounce of the greatness Spain keep in reserve. Two of our teams, one ticket to MetLife. Vamos España — the restraint must end in Dallas.

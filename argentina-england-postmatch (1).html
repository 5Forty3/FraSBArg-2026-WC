We predicted 1-1 with Argentina to edge it late, and warned England's imperative was to win inside 90. They led with 35 minutes to go — and bunkered. Argentina, and Messi, did the rest.

Yesterday Spain played the best match of their tournament. Today the world champions answered with the best of theirs — better even than those delirious final twenty minutes against Egypt. It was a complete team effort, a performance of relentless, suffocating siege football against eleven men who had decided to defend a one-goal lead with thirty-five minutes still to play. And yet, for all the collective excellence, it was Messi in every single frame. Messi in the build-up, Messi at the corner flag, Messi wriggling out of triple-teams, Messi lofting the ball into places no other footballer alive would have seen. Two assists in the last six minutes. A final reached. And something larger settled.

🐐 The debate is over — and it isn't close any more

For two decades a question has haunted Argentine football: who is the greatest of them all? And Maradona always held that special, unassailable place, because he had done the one thing Messi supposedly could not — he dragged a B-grade team to two World Cup finals, in 1986 and 1990, winning the first almost single-handedly. That was the trump card. That was the argument.

Tonight it became moot. Messi has gone one up on Maradona: this will be his THIRD World Cup final. 2014, when Germany broke Argentine hearts in extra time. 2022, when he finally lifted it. And now 2026 — back-to-back finals on top of it, the first Argentina have reached in consecutive World Cups since Maradona's own 1986-90. Should he start on Sunday, he becomes the only player in the history of the game to start three World Cup finals — joining Cafu as the only man to appear in three at all, and Cafu came off the bench in one of them. Not Pelé. Not Beckenbauer. Not Maradona. Nobody.

And he has done it while carrying a side even more dependent on him than Maradona's ever were — flawed, aged, structurally naive — at thirty-nine, leading the tournament's scoring charts, in a modern game infinitely more physical, more analysed, more designed to stop exactly what he does. He has done it while every opponent knows precisely what is coming and still cannot prevent it.

The Argentine debate is finished. And with it, so is the larger one. Lionel Messi is the greatest footballer the world has ever produced. There is nothing this little maestro cannot do on a football pitch. Tonight he created magic again, and again, and again — and made himself immortal.

Maradona's claim rested on carrying a lesser team to two finals. Messi has now reached a third — back-to-back, at thirty-nine, having already won one of them. The argument that protected Maradona for forty years has just been beaten at its own game, and then beaten by one more. — The greatest of all time, no longer debatable

🧊 The First Hour — a game of ice, and a single crack

For a long time this was not a football match but a wrestle. Neither side managed a single shot in the opening thirty minutes — the first time that has happened to start a World Cup match since 1966. The fouls came thick and constant (nineteen by half-time, twelve of them Argentina's), tempers frayed, and referee Ismail Elfath had to stop play to cool it down. Argentina were dragging England down to their level, and England were content to be dragged. Messi's first genuine spark came on 37 minutes — wriggling through multiple tackles before Anderson simply wiped him out and took the game's first yellow.

Then, at 55 minutes, came the only ten seconds all night in which England looked like a football team.

⚽ The Goal (55') — Gordon, and England's one clean moment

England · 1-0The only time England had control: one clean transition, one tap-in
England's transition & Rogers' cross Gordon's finish

Ten seconds of control, and it produced a goal. It came in transition — the one phase of the game England could reasonably hope to win — and, tellingly, it came down Argentina's problem flank. Anthony Gordon streaked into the space behind Molina, arrived at exactly the right moment on Morgan Rogers' wide cross, and tapped it home. It was clean, well-worked, and entirely deserved on the balance of a turgid hour — and it was the one lane Argentina's otherwise magnificent defence left ajar all night. Had England then gone looking for a second — pressed their advantage, kept Argentina honest, kept attacking that same right side — this might have been a very different evening. Instead, this proved to be the last meaningful attacking act England produced all night. They never created another great chance. They chose, instead, to defend for thirty-five minutes against Lionel Messi.

🚨 The coaching catastrophe — ten tall defenders and no imagination

What Thomas Tuchel did next will be discussed in England for years. With a one-goal lead and thirty-five minutes remaining, England did not merely sit back — they systematically dismantled their own capacity to attack. The goalscorer Gordon was withdrawn for a defender, Konsa. Declan Rice and Reece James were replaced by the 6'7" Dan Burn and the 6'4" Nico O'Reilly. The shape became a back five. By the end, England were effectively fielding a wall of tall defenders and nothing else — no outlet, no threat, no way of relieving even a moment of pressure. The consequence was arithmetic: 36% possession, 0.53 expected goals, five shots all match and only two on target. Most damning of all — Harry Kane and Jude Bellingham, who had scored twelve goals between them at this tournament, managed one shot between them tonight. Their stars were rendered non-existent, not by Argentina, but by their own manager's instructions.

And opposite him, the contrast could not have been sharper. Scaloni — so often criticised in these pages for his conservatism — sharpened his shape: Paredes off for the pacy Nico González on 64 minutes, De Paul on for Simeone, and Lautaro Martínez introduced to become the super-sub who won it. One coach added attackers to chase a game. The other added centre-backs to survive one. Only one of them was right.

🛡️ The Other Half of the Story — a rock-solid Argentine defence

For all the talk of Messi, this match was won as much at the back as it was in the final third, and that deserves saying plainly: Argentina's defence was rock solid. They did not merely contain England — they won the physical contest outright, taking something close to eighty per cent of the contested balls and producing tackle after impossible tackle. This was the aged, leaky back line that had shipped goals in every knockout round, and tonight it was immense.

The numbers tell it starkly. Harry Kane and Jude Bellingham — twelve goals between them at this World Cup, the first England pair ever to score five-plus each in a single tournament — managed one shot between them. England finished with five shots all night, two on target, and 0.53 expected goals. That is not an accident of Tuchel's bunkering alone; that is a defence that refused to be beaten. Cristian Romero was magnificent — aggressive, front-footed, winning duels he had no business winning — with Lisandro Martínez alongside him and Leandro Paredes screening in front, breaking up England's every attempt to play forward. And note who was doing the dirty work in midfield: Enzo and Paredes announced themselves with early fouls to knock England's midfielders off their rhythm, and by half-time Argentina had committed twelve of the game's nineteen fouls. It was cynical, it was streetwise, and it worked. Even Messi joined in — he won twelve duels against England, his most in a World Cup match since 2014, when he was twenty-seven.

Argentina · The Back LineRomero, Lisandro and Paredes suffocate England — but Molina is the one crack
The spine that won everything England's routes — blocked, except one

This is why England's stars were invisible — and it wasn't only Tuchel's fault. Argentina's spine was ferocious: Paredes screening ahead of Romero and Lisandro Martínez, winning roughly eighty per cent of the contested balls, and producing tackles that had no right to come off. Kane found nothing to feed on; Bellingham's celebrated late runs were tracked and swallowed. Every central lane was shut. But there was one genuine crack, and it is worth naming honestly: Nahuel Molina. The right-back lacks pace, and he was repeatedly caught wanting — and it is no coincidence that England's only goal came down that side, with Gordon streaking clear and arriving unmarked at the back post. Against an England side too timid to press the advantage, once was all it cost. Against Spain on Sunday, that flank is a far more frightening prospect — because Lamine Yamal lives there, and he will not stop at once.

🎼 The De Paul Effect — the minder arrives, and the ball starts flowing

The complexion of the match changed completely the moment Rodrigo De Paul came on. It is no accident that he is known as Messi's bodyguard and minder — his role in this side has never been about his own numbers. It is a job of service and protection: shadow the great man, do his running, absorb the fouls meant for him, and make sure the ball arrives where he wants it. He arrived and immediately began choreographing the flow of the ball toward Messi on the right, where the maestro could turn, face up, and begin his trademark work. Almost at once the chances came: De Paul's exquisite cross on 76 minutes found Mac Allister, whose header smacked the inside of the post. Three or four more followed, virtually all of them manufactured by Messi himself — inch-perfect balls into the box that a better, braver team would have converted every time. Mac Allister hit the woodwork twice. Nico González was denied by a fine diving Pickford save. Argentina were, for a spell, desperately unlucky.

This team is nothing without its magician — and everything with him. Three and four English defenders swarmed Messi all night, and still he found openings from impossible positions, conjuring chances out of angles that did not exist. — The one-man gravity of the Argentine attack

⚽ The Equaliser (85') — the short corner, and Messi's improvisation

Argentina · 1-1High balls weren't beating the giants — so Messi changed the method entirely
Messi's short corner & lay-off Enzo's strike

This is the goal that proves the point about Messi — not the assist itself, but the thinking behind it. Argentina had been lofting cross after cross into a box now populated by giants, and Messi read what everyone else was too slow to see: the high ball was never going to beat a forest of six-foot-seven defenders. So he improvised. He started taking the corners short — winning the ball back on the ground, drawing defenders out, and manufacturing a better angle from open play instead of gifting the tall men exactly what they were built for. And it worked immediately. Messi took the quick corner, got the return, drove infield toward the box, and laid it into the space outside the area where Enzo Fernández — who had blazed narrowly over from distance barely a minute earlier, and had been banging away at Pickford all afternoon — unleashed a thunderous right-footed strike that bent past the goalkeeper into the upper corner. The goal of his life. And its architect had solved the puzzle three moves earlier. The provider? Messi. Who else.

⚽ The Winner (90+2') — the cruellest irony of all

Argentina · 2-1Mac Allister hits the post — Messi keeps his head, resets, and lofts it perfectly
Mac Allister off the post Messi's cross Lautaro's header

And then the dagger — and the irony of it is almost too perfect. Mac Allister's shot smashed against the right post and stayed in play. Where a lesser player would have snatched at the rebound in the frenzy of stoppage time, Messi collected it and simply… waited. He kept possession, calm amid chaos, holding the ball long enough for the play to reset and for bodies to find their positions — and then lofted a cross of absurd precision to the back post. Lautaro Martínez, the substitute, rose and headed it into the left side of the net. Argentina had scored twice in seven minutes to win a World Cup semi-final. And here is the irony: England had loaded the pitch with giants precisely to win headers — 6'7", 6'4", a back five built for aerial war — and were beaten by a header. They had the height. They simply had no imagination to fight against such magic. You cannot defend a delivery that good with size alone. Someone has to think.

🎯 The prediction, and the reality

Our pre-match analysis predicted 1-1 with Argentina edging it late, and stated England's imperative plainly: "win it inside 90." They led with thirty-five minutes to play and chose instead to defend — the one strategy the analysis warned would fail. We also asked whether Scaloni would finally find a Plan B if Messi were caged. The answer was better than expected: Simeone did start (giving Argentina real width for the first time all tournament), and when it was needed, Scaloni added Nico González, De Paul and Lautaro rather than retreating. The Plan B arrived at last — and it won a World Cup semi-final.

📊 The Last Word

Argentina are in the World Cup final, and they got there by playing their finest football of the tournament — but more than that, they got there because they have Lionel Messi and nobody else does. It should not be forgotten, though, that this was won at both ends: Romero, Lisandro and Paredes were immense, winning something like eighty per cent of the contested balls and reducing Kane and Bellingham — twelve tournament goals between them — to a single shot. England had one clean moment in ninety-five minutes, found it down Molina's flank, and scored from it; then they built a fortress of tall men and invited the greatest player in history to solve them. He did, twice, in six minutes: first by realising that high balls would never beat giants and switching to short corners to manufacture the angle for Enzo's thunderbolt, then by collecting a rebound in stoppage-time bedlam, calmly resetting the play, and lofting a cross so perfect that even a forest of six-footers could only watch Lautaro head it home. Tuchel's bunker will be dissected for a generation. And the oldest debate in Argentine football is finished forever: Maradona reached two finals, in 1986 and 1990. Messi has now reached a third — back-to-back, at thirty-nine, having already won one — and on Sunday becomes the only player who has ever started three. He is not merely the greatest Argentine. He is the greatest of all time. Messi is now immortal — and one match from a perfect ending. Vamos Argentina.

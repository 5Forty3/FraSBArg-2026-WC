🌍 A thought before kick-off — does the World Cup need reimagining?

First, the good news for our project: the last of our quartet is in. We picked three of the four semi-finalists — a 75% hit rate — with Brazil the sole weak link, undone by Norway. And history was made: for the first time since FIFA rankings began in 1992, the top four ranked nations — Argentina, France, Spain and England — have all reached the semi-finals. Yet watching these games, a worry grows. Most of these giants already look tired — leg-weary, laboured, running on fumes. This expanded 48-team World Cup has added an extra round and an extra match, piled onto the end of a gruelling, unbroken club season. The spectacle is suffering for it: fewer moments of freshness, more attritional slogs decided by fatigue and fine margins. If the World Cup wishes to remain the greatest sporting event on earth, it may need to reimagine itself — the calendar, the load, the rest — before the world's best are reduced to shadows of themselves by the time the biggest matches arrive.

There is a particular kind of Argentine performance that thrills and disturbs in equal measure, and Kansas City delivered it in full. For seventy minutes and more, the defending champions were barely present in the match they were supposedly controlling — a goal to the good through an early set-piece, and then absent, passive, seemingly content to let Switzerland come. The Swiss, playing their first quarter-final in seventy years, sensed a monumental upset. And then football's cruel machinery intervened on the Europeans' behalf: a red card, harsh in its trigger and self-inflicted in its cause, reduced them to ten. From that moment Argentina laid siege — and eventually, through an Álvarez thunderbolt of outrageous audacity, they broke the wall. 3-1 flatters them enormously. This was a night that raised more questions about Argentina than it answered.

🎯 The prediction, and the reality

Our pre-match analysis predicted a 2-1 Argentine win "the hard way", warned that their four-midfielder system leaves them "unbalanced and exposed to the counter", and flagged that Switzerland's disciplined block and Kobel could make it "a very long night". All of it came to pass — the leaky defence conceded again, the Swiss counter drew level, and it took extra time and a moment of individual genius to settle it. The one twist no model could predict: a red card that turned a genuine upset bid into an eventual, laboured Argentine win.

⚽ The Opener (10') — Messi's corner, Mac Allister's head, against the run

Argentina · 1-0An inch-perfect Messi corner — and a record 10th World Cup assist
Messi's corner Mac Allister's header

Against the run of play, and of course it flowed from Messi. Switzerland had begun the brighter, but on ten minutes Argentina won a corner and the captain did the rest: an inch-perfect delivery from the right that Alexis Mac Allister needed only to nod towards goal. It was a moment of quiet history — Messi's record 10th career World Cup assist, the first player ever to reach double figures. For the first time in this knockout run, Argentina had the luxury of an early lead, and one imagined they might now play with freedom, seize the game, and pour forward. Instead, the opposite unfolded — and what followed was, for a lover of Latin American football, genuinely dispiriting.

⏳ The Riquelme Disease — Masters of Time, Who Forgot to Attack

What Argentina did with their lead was deploy the one art every Argentine generation inherits like a birthright: the control of the pace of the game. They slowed everything to a crawl, drained the tempo, and turned the match into a exercise in time-management. There is a lineage here — this is the nation that produced Juan Román Riquelme, arguably the greatest artist of the modern era, a man who did not so much play with time as bend it to his will, dictating the rhythm of ninety minutes with the ball glued to his boot. It is a beautiful, distinctly Argentine skill. But on this night they took the artistry to a self-defeating extreme: they slowed the game down so completely that they forgot the very purpose of attacking it. Switzerland dominated possession through the first half — without ever truly threatening — while Argentina simply sat back, defended, and let the clock tick. A one-goal lead became a hiding place rather than a springboard.

Argentina are such masters of slowing the game down — heirs to Riquelme, who controlled time itself on the pitch — that sometimes, like all artists lost in their craft, they take it too far. Tonight they slowed the match so thoroughly that they forgot the purpose of attack itself. — The genius and the self-sabotage of controlling time
Argentina · The StagnationEverything funnels to Messi — and without him on the ball, the team stops
Sideways, sterile possession around Messi

This is the structural truth this match laid bare — Argentina's greatest boon is also their greatest bane. Every Argentine move in the opposition half is oriented toward one aim: get the ball to Messi's feet. Often the team simply passes it around him, and around itself, with Messi drifting and apparently disinterested — until, suddenly, he receives, turns, and beguiles the entire defence. When the ball is at his feet, anything can happen; even magic. Three and four Swiss players surrounded him all night, and still he made the best moves, beating them at times and manufacturing the game's few clear openings. But the flip side is damning: this has reduced Argentina to something close to a one-trick pony. Shut Messi off — swarm him, as Switzerland did — and there is no evident Plan B. No second point of gravity, no alternative source of threat, no structural way to hurt you that doesn't route through the number ten.

⚽ The Equaliser (67') — Ndoye punishes the passivity

Switzerland · 1-1Rodríguez slips Ndoye in — and Dibu is finally beaten
Rodríguez's pass Ndoye's finish

It had been coming — and it was thoroughly deserved. In the second half Switzerland found belief. They kept attacking relentlessly, and Argentina, stunned, retreated ever deeper into their own corner. After forcing Emiliano Martínez into a couple of genuine saves, the Swiss broke through: Ricardo Rodríguez slipped a tidy pass to Dan Ndoye, who rolled a composed finish right under the goalkeeper's outstretched leg. 1-1, and no more than the Europeans merited. This is the point worth dwelling on: unlike the Cape Verde and Egypt games — where Argentina, whatever the scoreline, were the dominant side denied only by heroic goalkeeping or a parked bus — here they were simply second-best. They did not attack the ball. They did not try to possess it with any real intent. They had shut themselves off, and a good side punished them for it. For a team of this pedigree, it bordered on the shameful.

🟥 The Turning Point (72') — Embolo's needless dive, and the red

The Red CardA second yellow for simulation — a self-inflicted wound at the worst time

And then, football's fickle fortune swung decisively to the side that least deserved it in that moment. Five minutes after equalising, with all the momentum theirs, Switzerland self-destructed. Breel Embolo — who had been jostling and niggling with Romero and Lisandro Martínez all night — went down under a supposed Paredes challenge and was shown a second yellow for simulation after a VAR review. And here is the maddening part: it was a genuine dive, and a completely needless one. Paredes never touched him; the video was unambiguous. Worse, it was pointless — Embolo pulled off that stunt at the very moment his side was on top and Argentina were adrift, in a game being watched microscopically by VAR. There was no reason to go looking for a foul he didn't need. It was gamesmanship taken one fatal step too far, and it cost Switzerland their striker, their momentum, and ultimately the tie. A cruel, self-inflicted wound.

🎯 The Golazo (112') — Álvarez, the only way past ten men

Argentina · 2-1A curling thunderbolt from distance — the only way through the wall
Álvarez cuts in & curls the golazo

It took something extraordinary to break a ten-man Swiss wall defending for its life — and Julián Álvarez produced it. After Embolo's dismissal, Argentina laid siege to the Swiss goal for the rest of normal time and all of extra time — wave after wave, header after header, every route inside the box throttled by bodies. And that is exactly why the winning goal, when it came, could only have come from where it did: far outside the penalty area. Álvarez drifted in from the left and whipped a curling thunderbolt into the far top corner, beyond Kobel's reach — a strike of sheer audacity, power and precision that will be remembered long after the rest of this game is forgotten. It was the only way to score, because everything closer had been suffocated. Lautaro Martínez added a third in the 121st minute — a rebound from Almada's break as Switzerland finally had to chase the game — to make a desperately hard night look, on the scoreboard, almost comfortable. It was nothing of the sort.

🧩 The Plan-B Problem — Almada, width, and Scaloni's stubbornness

⚠️ The tactical critique — a team with no second idea

Here is what the siege exposed. As Argentina chased the ten-man wall, Scaloni eventually threw on four strikers to force the issue and avoid penalties — yet made no genuine tactical shift, no structural change to how the team actually creates. This is a side that plays with a four-man midfield and no natural width at all, because it is built, wholly and deliberately, around Messi. And the telling evidence came from the bench: when Thiago Almada came on, he immediately opened up the left side of the pitch — providing the width and directness that Ángel Di María once gave this team, and that it has sorely lacked. One wonders what a side with Almada (or a starting Lo Celso, or Nico Paz) might look like — with a second axis of threat, a reason for defenders to worry about something other than the number ten. But Scaloni refuses, and perhaps, with a magician in your midst, there is no other way: you build everything around him, and the De Pauls, Mac Allisters and Fernández become supporting artisans whose job is to feed the genius. There is simply no room for a second point of gravity. It is why, when Álvarez scored his belter, it was almost the only meaningful thing he did all night — a passenger, otherwise, in the Messi jigsaw.

Shut Messi off and Argentina have no Plan B — no second point of gravity, no alternative threat. Almada opened the left in minutes off the bench; imagine him starting. But with a magician in your midst, perhaps you simply build the whole cathedral around him, and everyone else becomes a bricklayer. — The price of building a team around one man

👴 Not Ageing — Aged

And so to the uncomfortable conclusion this tournament keeps pointing toward. This is not an ageing team. This is an aged team. Period. These are tired legs that have now dragged themselves through three brutal knockout ties — two of them going the full 120 minutes — on top of a long club season, in an expanded tournament that asks more than ever. By any rational measure, these weary limbs should not carry Argentina much further. And yet they keep defying the odds, keep walking the brink and surviving, propelled by something that does not show up in the physical data: an almost supernatural collective will, and the certainty that while Messi breathes, they are never beaten. It is magnificent and it is precarious in equal measure. The body says they are spent. The soul says otherwise. So far, in this tournament of the improbable, the soul keeps winning the argument.

📋 The Performers

Lionel Messi — The one constant. Quiet by his standards and swarmed by three or four all night, yet still the source of everything — the assist for Mac Allister and a record 10th WC assist. When the ball reaches his feet, magic remains possible.
Julián Álvarez — A passenger, then a hero. Anonymous in the Messi jigsaw for 110 minutes — then the audacious golazo that won the tie. Proof of quality, and of how little the system gives him otherwise.
Alexis Mac Allister — The early header. Took his moment from Messi's corner; a diligent, selfless shift in the engine room.
Thiago Almada (sub) — The width they lacked. Transformed the left the moment he arrived, teed up the third. A glimpse of a Plan B Scaloni won't start.
Dan Ndoye & Switzerland — Should have had more. The better side for long spells; Ndoye's goal was deserved. Undone by a self-inflicted red and Argentine resolve.
Breel Embolo — The costly dive. Combative all night, then a needless simulation for a second yellow that handed Argentina the tie. Gamesmanship one step too far.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 The Semi-Final — Can England Stop Messi & Co?

Argentina's reward is a semi-final against England in Atlanta — and on the evidence of the quarter-finals, it is a tie the champions will fancy, tired legs and all. Because England were, if anything, even less convincing: they laboured past Norway 2-1 in extra time in a performance so poor that their own manager, Thomas Tuchel, openly criticised the way his side played. For long spells Norway looked the better team; England were rescued by Bellingham's individual brilliance rather than any collective coherence. The question hangs heavy: can this England — hapless for long stretches, dependent on moments — really find a way to stop Messi and a side that, whatever its flaws, simply refuses to die?

Semi-Final · Atlanta, Jul 15Argentina's Messi-axis vs England's Bellingham-reliance

On paper, England have the tools — but they must show a coherence they have entirely lacked. The one clear advantage they hold is freshness: England won inside 120 minutes but expended less than an Argentina side that has now gone to extra time twice and been dragged to the limit three times. Fresh legs against aged ones could matter enormously in the Atlanta heat. But football is rarely so simple, and Argentina's edge is the intangible: a serial winner's DNA, a magician who needs only a half-yard, and Dibu Martínez waiting in any shootout. If England are to win, they must do what Switzerland nearly did — cage Messi, stay compact, and take their moments — but without the discipline collapsing into the passivity that Bellingham alone rescued against Norway.

The Key BattleEngland must swarm Messi — and pray their own creator delivers
The through-ball England must prevent

The tactical crux of the semi is the same question Switzerland posed — and nearly answered. England will have to collapse two or three bodies onto Messi whenever he receives, as everyone now does, to smother the game's single decisive player. Rice, Stones and Guéhi have the athleticism to try. But the danger of over-committing to Messi is the space it concedes behind — for the runs of Lautaro, Álvarez and the arriving midfielders — and Messi, even swarmed, retains the vision to find that pass, as he did all night against Switzerland. For England, the mirror-image question looms: with their own attack so reliant on Bellingham's individual moments and Kane's poaching, can they generate enough themselves against a side that, however aged, still knows how to win the biggest games? It has the makings of a classic — and, given the 2022 final, a tantalising sense of destiny about Argentina's march.

🏆 The Future — Three of Our Four, and a Familiar Feeling

Take stock of where our quartet stands: France, Spain and Argentina have all reached the semi-finals — three of our four, with only Brazil, the weak link, fallen. France and Spain meet each other in the upper-half semi, guaranteeing one of them the exit; Argentina stand alone in the lower half against England. It means the dream of a FraSBArg final is not just alive but likely — at least one of our teams is certain to reach the MetLife showpiece, and possibly two.

And for Argentina specifically, there is a familiar, almost eerie feeling building. This is a team that should, by every physical logic, be running on empty — aged, twice taken to the brink, dragging tired legs through an unforgiving schedule. And yet here they are, one win from another final, propelled by the same self-belief and the same magician that carried them in Qatar. Perhaps it is destined to be a repeat of 2022 — and perhaps the only force capable of stopping these aged, brilliant, brink-walking legends is not any opponent, but the simple, cruel arithmetic of exhaustion finally catching up with them. For now, though, they march on. They always seem to. Vamos Argentina.

📊 The Last Word

Argentina are into the semi-finals, and the scoreboard reads 3-1, but do not be fooled: this was a night that flattered them and exposed them in equal measure. For seventy minutes they were absent — masters of time who slowed the game into oblivion and forgot to attack it, heirs to Riquelme who mistook control for purpose. Switzerland, the better side for long spells, deserved their equaliser and might have caused the upset of the tournament, only to self-destruct through Embolo's needless dive. From there Argentina laid siege to ten men, and it took an Álvarez golazo of pure audacity — the only way past that wall — to break them. The deeper truths linger: a team utterly dependent on Messi with no Plan B, a coach who won't unlock the width Almada offers, and legs that are not ageing but aged. And still they win. Still they march. Next, England — hapless against Norway, slammed by their own manager — must find a way to stop them. On this evidence, that is a very tall order. Vamos Argentina — on the brink, as ever, and somehow still standing.

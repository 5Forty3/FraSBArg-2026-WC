The closest Argentina have been pushed since the 2022 final — and against the smallest nation ever to reach a World Cup knockout. A scare, a survival, and a warning.

🧭 The Story — Sublime, Then Ridiculous, Then Champions

This was a strangely disjointed symphony. For forty-five minutes Argentina were a study in clinical control — total possession, no space conceded, the ball won back the instant it was lost, Cape Verde pinned and gasping. And then, almost inexplicably, the champions seemed to shift down a gear and simply let go of the game. The opening quarter of the second half saw them cede possession, drop their intensity, and — literally walking rather than pressing — invite the smallest nation ever to reach a World Cup knockout to attack their goal. Cape Verde, with nothing to lose, accepted the invitation twice. Argentina swung from the sublime to the ridiculous and back again, needing an extra-time own goal from a Messi corner to finally see off the bravest of underdogs, 3-2. They march on. But they were given the fright of their lives.

🎚️ The theme of the night — a team shifting gears mid-match

The defining feature of this performance was its jarring inconsistency. Argentina found rhythm in some passages and threw it all away in others, as if changing gears within the same game — purring one moment, stalling the next. When they were good, they were untouchable: the first half was as complete a 45 minutes as they have played. When they switched off — invariably after taking the lead — they became passive, static and vulnerable, gifting a well-drilled, fearless opponent the space and belief to punish them. That oscillation, more than the scoreline, is the story of the night, and the source of both the reassurance and the alarm Argentina take from Miami.

⚽ Goal 1 (29') — Messi's sublime control and finish

Argentina · 1-0A magnetic touch with the outside of the left boot, then the dagger
Lisandro's pass into the box Messi's control & finish

Pure vision, pure genius — a goal from the sublime end of the spectrum. The breakthrough was a moment of the highest class. A raking pass was slid into the box — the assist coming, in fact, from centre-back Lisandro Martínez — and Messi did the rest with a touch that defied physics: a magnetic pick-up with the outside of his left boot that killed the ball dead and took him past his marker in a single motion, before he flicked a sublime finish past the onrushing Vozinha. It was Messi's seventh goal of the tournament and his record-extending 20th at World Cups, in his record 30th appearance — the first player ever to score in eight consecutive World Cup games. At that moment, with Argentina in total command, the only question seemed to be the margin. Football, and Cape Verde, had other ideas.

📉 The Weakness Exposed — When Argentina Dropped the Gears

Argentina · The FlawLead taken, intensity gone — the passive lull that let Cape Verde in
Cape Verde attack the space Duarte's equaliser

The single most alarming aspect of the night — and it is a real, recurring flaw. Having controlled the first half with total authority, Argentina came out for the second and, inexplicably, lost intensity, dropped their pace, and surrendered their coordination. They stopped pressing, they conceded possession cheaply, and — as you'd notice from the stands — they were walking, not running, allowing a Cape Verde side that had been suffocated for 45 minutes to suddenly find acres of space. On 59 minutes it cost them: Ryan Mendes fed Deroy Duarte, who turned and drilled low inside the far post for a historic equaliser. This was the team that had been defensively immaculate for three-and-a-half games simply zoning out — forgetting both its pressing intensity and its own possession football. It is baffling, and it is dangerous. The lulls, especially after scoring, are the fault line that better teams will exploit ruthlessly.

⚽ Goal 2 (91') — Lisandro Martínez's extra-time piledriver

Argentina · 2-1Straight from the restart of extra time — a corner, a thunderbolt
Messi's corner Lisandro's strike

Regulation ended 1-1, Vozinha having produced save after save — including a stunning stop to deny Messi's quickly-taken free-kick that was destined for the top corner — to force the extra half-hour. And Argentina, as if remembering who they were, struck almost immediately. Barely two minutes into extra time, from a Messi corner, Lisandro Martínez rifled a left-footed piledriver high past Vozinha — the centre-back with a goal and an assist on the night, an unlikely hero. It should have been the decisive blow. Against this Cape Verde, nothing was decisive until the final whistle.

⚽ Goal 3 for CV (103') — Cabral's stunner, and the wall that was Vozinha

Cape Verde · 2-2A goal for the ages — and a goalkeeping masterclass at the other end
Cabral's cut-in & curl

Nothing to lose, and it showed in a goal of pure brilliance. Cape Verde's second was the embodiment of their fearless spirit. Sidny Lopes Cabral cut inside from the left edge of the area and bent an unstoppable right-footed curler into the far corner — a strike good enough to win any match, and one that may stand forever as the greatest goal in Cape Verdean history. It made it 2-2 in extra time and had Argentina staring at either penalties or the greatest upset the World Cup has ever seen. And credit must go, too, to the man at the other end: the 40-year-old Vozinha, a second-division goalkeeper, produced eight saves — several of them stunners that would have beaten most keepers nine times in ten — denying Messi on three or four massive openings. For 110 minutes, the fairytale was alive because of him.

⚽ The Winner (111') — Messi's corner, Romero's head, the cruellest touch

Argentina · 3-2The wand of a left foot delivers; a header, a deflection, heartbreak
Messi's corner Romero header Borges deflection (OG)

With penalties looming and the tension unbearable, Argentina finally found the decisive moment — and, fittingly, it came from the wand of a left foot that had been denied all night. Messi curled an inch-perfect corner into the box, Cristian Romero rose to meet it, and the ball flicked off Cape Verde's Diney Borges and past the helpless Vozinha — an own goal to break the underdogs' hearts. It was credited to Romero before the official change, but the author, in truth, was Messi and his set-piece delivery. Even then Argentina were not safe; Emiliano Martínez had to make two smart late saves to preserve the win. But it was enough. The champions were through — by the skin of their teeth.

📋 The Performers

Lionel Messi — Sublime, and denied. The outside-of-the-boot goal, the corner for the winner, but also 3-4 huge chances spurned (mostly to Vozinha). 20th WC goal, 8 straight games. Genius amid the chaos.
Lisandro Martínez — Unlikely hero. An assist for Messi's opener and a thunderous extra-time goal of his own. Immense at both ends.
Vozinha (CPV) — Otherworldly. Eight saves, several jaw-dropping. The 40-year-old second-division keeper was the reason the fairytale lasted 110 minutes.
Sidny Lopes Cabral (CPV) — A goal for eternity. The greatest goal in his nation's history. Fearless.
The Argentina midfield — Zoned out. Immaculate in the first half, passive and static in the second. The lulls that nearly cost everything.
Lionel Scaloni — Caught out. For the first time in years looked short of answers as the game slipped — predictable changes, no clear contingency. A rare off night.
🥈 The Silver Lining — This Is How Champions Win

For all the alarm, there is a genuine and important positive to carry forward: this was a tough, scrappy, hard-fought victory — and that is precisely how Argentina, and most World Cup winners, actually win. The opponents came back from a deficit not once but twice; penalties loomed on the horizon; and still Argentina refused to fold and found a way to win the match. One could sense throughout that whenever they truly chose to, they could shift gears and score at will, even against a deep, disciplined block and a goalkeeper in the form of his life. That knowledge — that the class is always there to be summoned — is the mark of a champion. World Cups are rarely won by overwhelming every opponent; they are won by grinding through nights exactly like this. Barring the odd anomaly — Brazil in 1970, France in 2018 — the trophy goes to the team that learns to win ugly. On that measure, Argentina passed a test they didn't know they were sitting.

⚠️ The Real Weakness — The Lulls, and a Contingency Plan

But the concern is just as real, and it must be addressed. The way Argentina lost intensity and dropped their pace, action and coordination for long passages — especially after scoring — is a genuine flaw. It was as if they zoned out, and a side defensively immaculate for three-and-a-half games suddenly folded, forgetting its own possession football entirely. Most troubling was that, for the first time in years, Scaloni himself looked short of answers — caught unaware by the shift in momentum and reaching for the same predictable changes rather than a fix. He needs a contingency plan now: a way to arrest these lulls and, crucially, to use his squad depth more decisively — finding roles for the likes of Lo Celso, Nico Paz and even Álvarez to inject control and penetration when the rhythm drains away. The talent to win the tournament is unquestionable. But if these passages of passivity are not plugged, a better side than Cape Verde will not let Argentina off the hook.

🔮 The Future — Egypt Next, and the Questions to Answer

Argentina · The FixUse the bench to kill the lulls — control & penetration off the seat
Fresh legs re-impose control Álvarez penetration

The contingency Scaloni must build. The antidote to the lulls is not to defend deeper but to re-impose control through the ball — and Argentina's bench is loaded with exactly the profiles to do it. When the rhythm drains, introducing a Lo Celso or a Nico Paz to keep possession ticking and calm the tempo, alongside Julián Álvarez to run beyond and stretch a tiring block, gives Scaloni a proactive lever rather than a reactive one. The Messi–Álvarez axis from Qatar is right there to be rekindled. The lesson from Miami is that Argentina cannot simply "manage" a lead by sitting off; they must keep playing. With the right in-game changes, the passivity becomes a passing choice, not a fatal drift.

Argentina advance to a Round-of-16 tie with Egypt, who edged Australia on penalties, in Atlanta on Tuesday — a meeting that pits Messi against his great contemporary rival Mohamed Salah, even if Salah's own tournament has been hampered by fitness. On paper it is eminently winnable; Egypt are organised but limited going forward, and Argentina's quality should tell. But this performance is a warning that no tie can be taken lightly, and that Argentina's own lapses are a bigger threat than most opponents. If the champions again take a lead and then disengage, even a functional Egypt side carries enough to punish them.

Look further, and the challenges sharpen. Waiting in a potential quarter-final is Colombia — who dismissed Ghana with a comfortable, dominant 1-0 and possess, in a rhythmic possession game and genuine attacking talent, exactly the kind of quality that could exploit an Argentine lull far more clinically than Cape Verde managed. This is where the silver lining and the weakness meet: Argentina have proved they can win ugly and summon their class when it matters, which is the champion's gift — but they have also shown a recurring passivity that a Colombia, and beyond them the France or Spain lurking in the other half, would punish without mercy. The talent says they can retain the World Cup. The lulls say they must fix something first. Scaloni has a week, and a bench full of solutions, to find the answer. Vamos Argentina.

📊 The Last Word

A disjointed symphony, and a survival for the ages. Argentina were, by turns, sublime and ridiculous — imperious for a first half graced by a Messi goal of pure genius, then passive and static enough to let the smallest nation ever to reach a World Cup knockout twice haul themselves level, the second time with a goal for eternity. It took a Messi corner, a Romero header and the cruellest of deflections to finally settle it in extra time, with Vozinha's eight-save masterclass ensuring Cape Verde left as heroes. The silver lining is real and reassuring: this is how champions win — ugly, hard, refusing to fold — and the sense that Argentina can shift gears and score whenever they truly choose to never quite left. But so is the warning: the lulls, the zoning out, and a rare night where even Scaloni looked without answers must be fixed. Egypt await, and beyond them tougher tests still. The champions march on — chastened, alive, and with work to do. Vamos Argentina.
